001ISD 2026 Keynote

What this slide shows

The title carries the whole thesis in one line: recommendation, search and conversation, three interfaces that were built as separate products by separate teams, are converging on one underlying stack in real-world systems. The two logos name where the evidence in this talk comes from: the Faculty of Information Technology at the Czech Technical University in Prague, and Recombee, the recommendation company founded there.

Concepts worth knowing

  • Search is the interface where you type what you want and get a ranked list of results. Recommendation is the interface where you type nothing and the system proposes items from what it knows about you. Conversation is the interface where you ask in sentences and get an answer composed from evidence the system found. This talk claims that all three end in the same operation: selecting a few items from a catalogue and putting them in order.
  • A catalogue is whatever the system can return: products, articles, films, courses, documents, events. A ranking is an ordered subset of it.

The argument

A keynote for people who build information systems needs a thesis they can hold onto for forty minutes. This one is deliberately narrow: not "AI changes everything", but "three things you probably run as three systems are becoming one, and here is what that costs and what it gets wrong". Everything that follows either builds that claim, complicates it, or draws a consequence from it.

Evidence and further reading

  • Recombee Research — the lab's publications, open-source projects and the CTU collaboration that the talk draws on.
  • RecombeeLab at CTU FIT — the joint laboratory with the Department of Applied Mathematics.
TakeawayWhatever the interface, the system underneath chooses a few things from a catalogue and orders them. Keep that picture; every later slide is a variation on it.

003How information retrieval evolved over time?

What this slide shows

Four eras of information retrieval: retrieving records with Boolean queries; ranking documents with BM25 and PageRank; learning representations with word2vec and BERT; and generating responses with retrieval-augmented generation and generative search. Two questions close the slide: is generative AI part of information retrieval, and what exactly is IR in 2026?

Concepts worth knowing

  • Boolean retrieval returns every document that satisfies a logical condition ("contains tax AND deadline") in no particular order. It matches; it does not rank.
  • BM25 is the classic ranking formula: a document scores higher the more often it contains the query's words, weighted by how rare those words are across the collection, and adjusted for document length. It is still a strong baseline thirty years on.
  • PageRank scores a web page by the links pointing at it, weighted by the importance of the linking pages, so that authority, not just text, orders results.
  • An embedding (also "representation" or "vector") is a list of numbers that a model assigns to a word, a document, a product or a person, arranged so that similar things get nearby lists. Once everything is a vector, "similar" becomes a geometric distance a computer can compute quickly. word2vec did this for words in 2013; BERT did it in 2018 for words in context, so that "bank" near "river" and "bank" near "loan" get different vectors.
  • Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is the pattern where a language model is given documents found by a retriever and writes its answer from them, rather than from memory alone.

The argument

The eras are defined by what the system could score, not by technology fashion. Boolean systems matched; probabilistic ranking ordered; learned representations turned similarity into geometry; the generative era retrieves evidence and composes an answer over it. The observation that matters for the rest of the talk is that the last step did not replace retrieval, it wrapped it: a generated answer still stands on a ranked list of evidence. The two closing questions are answered by the talk itself.

Evidence and further reading

  • Mikolov et al., 2013 — Efficient Estimation of Word Representations in Vector Space — word2vec.
  • Devlin et al., 2018 — BERT — contextual representations from one pretrained model.
  • Lewis et al., 2020 — Retrieval-Augmented Generation — where the fourth era starts.
  • Robertson et al., 1994 — Okapi at TREC-3 — BM25.
TakeawayGeneration did not replace retrieval; it sits on top of it. In 2026, information retrieval is still the selection and ordering of items, whatever the interface then does with them.

004Three interfaces, one ranking problem

What this slide shows

Three mock-ups side by side, each with numbered results: a search box returning ranked pages, a homepage returning ranked items for a person, and a conversational assistant returning ranked documents or events before it composes an answer. The closing line: the interface changes; the decision remains, what should come first.

Concepts worth knowing

  • Relevance is the degree to which an item serves the current need; ranking is ordering candidates by an estimate of it. In search the need is the query; in recommendation it is inferred from the person's history and context; in conversation it is the question and the dialogue so far.
  • A candidate is an item the system considers returning. Almost every real system works in two steps: first pull a manageable set of candidates out of a huge catalogue, then order that set carefully. Later slides call the first step retrieval or candidate generation and the second ranking.

The argument

This is the operational model the rest of the talk relies on. Whatever the surface, the job underneath is to choose a handful of items from a catalogue and put them in order. Even the conversational answer is a ranking with a sentence wrapped around it. The claim is deliberately modest: ranking is the shared subroutine, while objectives, evaluation and economics stay different from one interface to the next, and the second half of the talk is about exactly those differences. A fourth interface, an agent acting on a person's behalf, returns at the end and asks the same question for a new kind of requester.

Evidence and further reading

  • Gong et al., 2023 — An Unified Search and Recommendation Foundation Model for Cold-Start Scenario — one production model at Alipay serves both search and recommendation, treating recommendation as search with an empty query.
  • Hou et al. — BLaIR: Bridging Language and Items for Retrieval and Recommendation — one table of item vectors consumed by sequential recommendation, collaborative filtering and product search alike.
  • D'Amico et al., 2026 — GLIDE at Spotify — a conversational instruction whose output is a ranked list of catalogue items.
TakeawayIf you design an information system with a search box, a personalised homepage and an assistant, you have one ranking problem with three front ends, not three problems.

005History of Search, Recommender systems and Conversational AI

What this slide shows

Four lineages in a table: classical information retrieval (Luhn, Salton, BM25), web search (PageRank, HITS), recommender systems (GroupLens, Amazon's item-to-item filtering, matrix factorization) and conversational interfaces (ELIZA, seq2seq, the Transformer, RAG). For each, the signal it added and the scope of the cited work. The closing line orders the signals in time: term statistics, links, user–item behaviour, learned sequence context, retrieval-grounded generation.

Concepts worth knowing

  • The vector space model (Salton, 1975) represents both documents and queries as vectors of word weights and scores by the angle between them. It is the ancestor of every embedding method; what changed later is that the vectors are learned rather than counted.
  • Collaborative filtering recommends items from the behaviour of many users: people who liked what you liked tend to like what you have not seen yet. It needs no description of the items at all, only who interacted with what.
  • Matrix factorization is the classic way to do that at scale: the large, mostly empty table of users by items is approximated by two small tables of vectors, one per user and one per item, so that a user's predicted interest in an item is the dot product of the two vectors.
  • Cold start is the failure mode of collaborative methods: a new user or a new item has no interactions yet, so there is nothing to learn from.
  • seq2seq models (2014) read one sequence and write another, the original neural machine translation; the Transformer (2017) is the architecture that does this with attention instead of recurrence and underlies every modern language model.

The argument

Four communities added four different signals to the same operation, and each new signal came with a new candidate set: documents, then pages, then items for a person, then evidence for an answer. Salton's model is the hinge: it established that documents and queries are vectors, but the similarity stayed lexical, word for word. Learned embeddings, four decades later, gave all four lineages the same representational machinery, which is why they could converge at all.

Evidence and further reading

  • Luhn, 1957 — A Statistical Approach to Mechanized Encoding and Searching of Literary Information and Salton, Wong and Yang, 1975 — A Vector Space Model for Automatic Indexing.
  • Robertson et al., 1994 — Okapi at TREC-3 — BM25.
  • Brin and Page, 1998 — The Anatomy of a Large-Scale Hypertextual Web Search Engine and Kleinberg, 1999 — Authoritative Sources in a Hyperlinked Environment.
  • Resnick et al., 1994 — GroupLens, Linden, Smith and York, 2003 — Amazon.com recommendations, Koren, Bell and Volinsky, 2009 — Matrix Factorization Techniques for Recommender Systems; Schein et al., 2002 measured the cold-start case.
  • Weizenbaum, 1966 — ELIZA, Sutskever et al., 2014 — Sequence to Sequence Learning, Vaswani et al., 2017 — Attention Is All You Need, Lewis et al., 2020 — RAG.
TakeawayThe four fields grew apart because each optimised a different signal. They could grow back together only once everything, words, documents, items and people, became vectors in the same kind of space.

006Learning to rank added a learned ordering layer

What this slide shows

Two columns. On the left, classical retrieval: BM25 or TF-IDF sparse vectors, cosine similarity, and a hand-set weight α that mixes the two scores into one. On the right, pointwise learning to rank as linear regression: the same signals become features x(q,d) of a query–document pair, a weight vector w is fitted from relevance labels by squared error, and the learned model reranks the top-k candidates that BM25 found. The comparison table makes the shift explicit: a hand-set α becomes learned coefficients; "retrieve and sort" becomes "rerank what the first stage found".

Concepts worth knowing

  • TF-IDF weights a word in a document by how often it appears there (term frequency) and how rare it is overall (inverse document frequency). A sparse vector is one with a slot for every word in the vocabulary, almost all of them zero; a document is a sparse vector of its word weights.
  • Cosine similarity is the angle between two vectors: 1 when they point the same way, 0 when they share nothing. It is the standard way to compare a query vector with a document vector.
  • Learning to rank (LTR) means training a model on examples of queries with documents judged relevant or not, so that it learns how to combine many signals into one ordering. Pointwise LTR scores each document on its own; the alternatives are on the next slide.
  • Top-k means the k best-scoring candidates from a first, cheap stage; a reranker is a more expensive model applied only to those. Recall is the share of the truly relevant items that made it into the candidate set; a reranker cannot recover what the first stage missed.

The argument

This is the first mechanism slide, and it introduces the pattern the whole stack still uses: a cheap first stage that preserves recall, and a learned second stage that improves the ordering by combining heterogeneous signals. The limitation on the slide is structural: however good the reranker, it only reorders what it was given. That is why candidate generation is a topic of its own later, and why production teams measure retrieval quality and ranking quality separately.

Evidence and further reading

  • Robertson et al., 1994 — Okapi at TREC-3 — the lexical first stage the learned layer sits on.
  • Learning to rank as a discipline goes back to RankNet (Burges et al., 2005) and LambdaMART; the slide keeps to the pointwise linear case because it is the one you can rebuild from a regression you already know.
  • Karpukhin et al., 2020 — Dense Passage Retrieval — the later step where the first stage itself becomes learned; the retrieve-then-rerank shape survives it.
TakeawayReal ranking systems are two stages: a fast filter that must not miss, and a learned ranker that must order well. Most of what looks like "AI search" is a better second stage on top of a very old first one.

007The model is only half of learning to rank

What this slide shows

Two independent choices. The scoring function can be a linear regression, gradient-boosted trees (LambdaMART) or a neural network. Separately, the training data decides what the model can learn: an itemwise cross-entropy loss learns one relevance probability from (query, document, relevant?) triples; a pairwise loss such as RankNet learns one score difference from (query, preferred document, other document); a listwise loss such as ListNet learns a distribution over a whole candidate list from graded labels. A worked example on the right computes the margin between a relevant and an irrelevant document for the query "gift for my wife" and the loss it produces.

Concepts worth knowing

  • A loss function is the number a model is trained to make small; it defines what "wrong" means. Choosing the loss is choosing the question the model answers.
  • Cross-entropy is the loss for predicting a probability: it punishes confident wrong answers heavily. Used itemwise it asks "is this document relevant, yes or no".
  • A pairwise loss looks at two documents for the same query and asks only which should come first; it never learns how relevant either one is on its own. A listwise loss looks at the whole list at once and is closest to the metrics people actually report.
  • Labels are the judgements the model learns from. Human relevance judgements are expensive, so systems often use clicks instead, and clicks are biased: people click what was shown near the top (position bias) and cannot click what was never shown (exposure bias).

The argument

Practitioners argue about model families and forget that the labels decide what the model can learn. A pairwise example supervises exactly one score difference; it says nothing about absolute relevance, and it needs relative preferences rather than yes/no judgements. Read the training-data column before the model column: that is where production ranking projects most often go wrong, because click-derived labels carry position and exposure bias whichever model consumes them.

Evidence and further reading

  • RankNet and LambdaMART (Burges and colleagues, Microsoft Research) are the canonical references for pairwise and listwise gradient-boosted ranking; ListNet is Cao et al., 2007.
  • Mansoury, 2021 — Understanding and Mitigating Multi-Sided Exposure Bias in Recommender Systems — why labels derived from what was shown are biased by the showing.
  • Chen et al. — Bias and Debias in Recommender System: A Survey — a taxonomy of where those biases enter.
TakeawayBefore asking which model to use, ask what your labels actually say. The loss function turns labels into a question, and a ranking model can only answer the question it was asked.

008Transformers in search

What this slide shows

Two ways to use a Transformer for ranking. A ranking Transformer, or cross-encoder, reads the query and the document together and emits one relevance score, typically from the [CLS] position; the two texts attend to each other inside the encoder. ColBERT encodes query and document separately into one contextual vector per token and scores by late interaction: for each query token, the maximum similarity over the document's tokens, summed. The figure contrasts the two pipelines.

Concepts worth knowing

  • A Transformer is the neural architecture behind modern language models. Its core operation, attention, lets every token of the input weigh every other token when computing its representation; the output is one contextual vector per token.
  • A token is the unit a language model reads, roughly a word or a piece of a word. [CLS] is a special first token whose output vector is used to summarise the whole input.
  • A cross-encoder puts query and document into the model together; it is the most accurate way to score a pair and the slowest, because every pair costs a full pass through the model. A bi-encoder (or two-tower model) encodes them separately, so documents can be encoded once, in advance.
  • Late interaction is ColBERT's middle way: documents are encoded in advance token by token, and the only work at query time is comparing the query's few token vectors with the stored ones. MaxSim is that comparison: for each query token, the best-matching document token.
  • An index is a data structure that makes finding candidates fast without scanning everything; here, a store of precomputed token vectors.

The argument

The cross-encoder is the most accurate thing you can do with a Transformer and the least deployable: it can only rerank a short list. ColBERT's move is to keep token-level matching while pushing the document side out of the query path, so the per-query cost is one small encoding plus cheap comparisons. That is the search-side instance of a pattern the recommender side had used for years, precompute the item side and pay per request only for the cheap interaction, and it is the mechanical reason the two stacks could share machinery.

Evidence and further reading

  • Vaswani et al., 2017 — Attention Is All You Need — the encoder both variants build on.
  • Khattab and Zaharia, 2020 — ColBERT — late interaction over per-token embeddings.
  • Reimers and Gurevych, 2019 — Sentence-BERT — why cross-encoders cannot be searched: finding the most similar pair among 10,000 sentences costs about 50 million passes, roughly 65 hours on one GPU, against seconds for a bi-encoder plus vector search.
  • Formal, Piwowarski and Clinchant, 2021 — SPLADE and Chen et al., 2024 — BGE M3-Embedding — the same backbone can also feed a classic inverted index, or emit dense, sparse and multi-vector representations at once; the index becomes a deployment choice.
TakeawayAccuracy and deployability pull against each other in Transformer ranking, and the resolution is always the same: do the expensive work on the catalogue in advance, and keep the per-request work small.

009Search became a personalized retrieval problem

What this slide shows

Two neural signals combine: query relevance from ColBERT-style token matching, and user preference from item and user vectors learned from interaction histories. The figure shows the shared space: a person's history produces a user vector; recommendation retrieves nearby items directly, while search shifts the same vector with a query before retrieving. The combined score is ColBERT(q,d) plus λ times the cosine similarity between the user and the document, with λ chosen on a held-out set per task.

Concepts worth knowing

  • A user embedding is a vector that summarises a person's behaviour so far, learned so that it sits near the items they engage with. Once users and items live in one space, "what would this person like" becomes "which item vectors are nearest".
  • Nearest-neighbour search finds the vectors closest to a given one; approximate nearest-neighbour (ANN) indexes do it in milliseconds over millions of items by accepting a small chance of missing the exact best match.
  • A hyperparameter such as λ is a knob set by the engineer rather than learned; a held-out set is data kept aside from training so that the knob is tuned on examples the model has not seen.

The argument

This is the convergence claim made concrete. Search and recommendation differ in the request, an explicit query versus an implicit profile, not in the representation. Two sources back it at different scales: Alipay trained one search-and-recommendation model across seven of its domains, and the BLaIR benchmark feeds one frozen item-vector table into sequential recommendation, collaborative filtering and product search. One qualification belongs with the claim: this describes logged-in product and site search, in commerce, news and super-apps; public web search has no profile and much stricter privacy constraints.

Evidence and further reading

  • Khattab and Zaharia, 2020 — ColBERT — the query-relevance term.
  • Gong et al., 2023 — An Unified Search and Recommendation Foundation Model for Cold-Start Scenario — Alipay: one scorer f(user, query, item), with the query set to empty for recommendation.
  • Hou et al. — BLaIR — one embedding table, three consumers, on 571 million Amazon reviews.
  • Rajesh et al., 2025 — PinFM — a 20-billion-parameter model of user activity pretrained once and fine-tuned per surface at Pinterest.
TakeawayIn a logged-in product, search is a recommender with a query attached: the same user vector, the same item vectors, one extra term in the score.

010A query is now a hypothesis about intent

What this slide shows

Semantic search as a four-step pipeline: complete the typed prefix into candidate queries (next-token prediction), understand the intent with a contextual encoder, retrieve lexical and semantic candidates (BM25 plus ColBERT), and answer with a response conditioned on the retrieved evidence (RAG). The closing line: search no longer matches a string; it predicts intent, retrieves evidence and composes an answer or a ranked list.

Concepts worth knowing

  • Query completion is the suggestion list under a search box; it is a small language model predicting how the typed prefix is likely to continue, given locale and session, and it is itself a recommender.
  • Intent is what the person wants, as opposed to what they typed; "jaguar price" is a car for one user and an animal print for another, and context decides.
  • Hybrid retrieval runs a lexical retriever (BM25, exact words) and a semantic retriever (vectors, meaning) side by side and merges the candidates. Each catches what the other misses: exact product codes on one side, paraphrases on the other.
  • Grounding means an answer is tied to specific retrieved evidence rather than produced from the model's memory. A grounded answer can still be wrong; it can, however, be checked.

The argument

Each step reuses a mechanism from the previous slides: the "semantic" in semantic search is not one model but a pipeline of the same components the recommender uses. Hybrid retrieval stays in the picture on purpose, because BM25 preserves exact terms that dense encoders blur and remains a hard baseline even for the newest retrievers. The last step opens the conversational section: an answer composed over retrieved evidence is grounded, but grounding is not correctness, which slide 17 takes up.

Evidence and further reading

  • Sutskever et al., 2014 — seq2seq and Vaswani et al., 2017 — the Transformer — completion and understanding.
  • Mikolov et al., 2013 — word2vec — the static-vector baseline that contextual encoders replaced.
  • Robertson et al., 1994 — BM25 and Khattab and Zaharia, 2020 — ColBERT — hybrid retrieval.
  • Lewis et al., 2020 — RAG; Wu et al. — Retrieval-Augmented Generation for NLP: A Survey — how retrieved evidence is fused into a model and what goes wrong.
TakeawayA modern search box is a chain of predictions: what you meant, what matches it, and what to say about it. Every link in that chain is a model that can be measured and replaced on its own.

011LLM inference is constrained by memory and scheduling

What this slide shows

Transformer inference has two phases: prefill processes the prompt in parallel, decode produces one token per sequential step. The table turns that into capacity arithmetic for Llama 2 70B in 16-bit precision: about 140 GB of weights (70 billion parameters times two bytes), about 320 KiB of key-value cache per token, so about 1.25 GiB per 4,096-token request; prefill attention that grows with the square of the sequence length, which FlashAttention makes memory-efficient without removing the arithmetic; decode throughput that improves with batching at the cost of tail latency, which Orca addresses by scheduling at the granularity of single steps; and fragmented cache memory that PagedAttention fixes, with a reported 2–4× throughput gain over earlier systems at comparable latency. The right column lists the limits of the usual mitigations: 4-bit weights cut the footprint to about 35 GB but add quantisation error; speculative decoding saves decode steps only when a small draft model guesses well; continuous batching raises utilisation but latency still depends on arrival rate and output length.

Concepts worth knowing

  • Parameters are the learned numbers inside a model; 70 billion of them at two bytes each is 140 GB that must sit in GPU memory before the first token is produced.
  • Prefill reads the whole prompt at once; decode then writes the answer one token at a time, each step depending on the previous one, which is why long answers are slow no matter how fast the hardware.
  • The KV cache (key-value cache) stores, for every token seen so far, the intermediate values attention needs, so they are not recomputed at every step. It grows with the length of the conversation: here 320 KiB for every token, over a gigabyte for one long request.
  • FLOPs are floating-point operations, the currency of compute; latency is how long one request waits, throughput how many requests a system completes per second. Batching many requests together raises throughput and, past a point, latency.
  • Quantisation stores weights with fewer bits (4 instead of 16) to save memory at some cost in accuracy. Speculative decoding lets a small, fast model propose several tokens that the large model then verifies in one step.

The argument

This is the cost axis seen from the language-model side, and it works in arithmetic rather than benchmarks on purpose. The KV-cache figure is the one to remember: 80 layers, keys and values, 8 key-value heads of dimension 128, two bytes each, is 327,680 bytes per token, so a single long conversation holds more than a gigabyte of state per request. That state is what the serving techniques on slide 23 cache, reuse and deduplicate. Treat the table as capacity planning: any real number depends on the index, the hardware and the recall target.

Evidence and further reading

  • Touvron et al., 2023 — Llama 2 — the model whose sizes the arithmetic uses.
  • Dao et al., 2022 — FlashAttention — exact attention with far less memory traffic.
  • Yu et al., 2022 — Orca — iteration-level scheduling for generative serving.
  • Kwon et al., 2023 — PagedAttention — paged KV cache; the 2–4× throughput figure.
  • Frantar et al., 2022 — GPTQ — 4-bit post-training quantisation and its error.
  • Leviathan, Kalman and Matias, 2022 — Fast Inference from Transformers via Speculative Decoding.
TakeawayA language model on the request path is a memory problem before it is an accuracy problem: 140 GB to load, more than a gigabyte of state per long conversation, and one sequential step per output token.

012Structure can beat parameter count on the right task

What this slide shows

Three results where structure buys more than parameters. PrefixMem at Pinterest: a 0.6-billion-parameter model with a dedicated encoder for semantic IDs beats a 4-billion one without, with deepest-level accuracy up 46 % relative. SPLADE: a sparse retriever that runs on a classic inverted index, trained from the same BERT as its dense sibling, slightly beats it, 0.322 against 0.312 MRR@10. CompresSAE, from Recombee Labs: dense embeddings projected into a high-dimensional, sparsely activated space give 12× fewer parameters at near-uncompressed quality, with two inference modes, a fast one in the sparse space and an exact one in the reconstructed dense space. The right column shows one schematic item: eight dense values become three active coordinates out of sixteen, and back.

Concepts worth knowing

  • Dense vectors have a value in every position; sparse vectors are mostly zeros and store only the positions that are active. Sparse vectors are cheaper to store and can be searched with an inverted index, the same structure a keyword search engine uses: for each active coordinate, the list of items that have it.
  • A semantic ID is a short code, a few small integers, assigned to an item from its content so that similar items get similar codes; slides 16–17 return to why that matters. An encoder is the part of a model that turns an input into a vector.
  • MRR@10 (mean reciprocal rank) is a ranking metric: if the correct answer is first the score is 1, if second 1/2, if third 1/3, and 0 if it is not in the top ten; averaged over queries.
  • An autoencoder is a model trained to compress its input and reconstruct it; a sparse autoencoder is one whose compressed form is a wide vector with only a few active entries.

The argument

Sometimes a bigger model wins, and the claim on the slide is the narrower one: on a constrained task, the right structure can dominate parameter count. Pinterest's result reuses a lesson from multimodal language models, that a non-language input wants its own encoder, for semantic IDs. CompresSAE is the lab's version of the same idea for retrieval: sparsity as a design decision rather than a compression applied afterwards, with a dial between speed and exactness that can be turned at run time.

Evidence and further reading

  • Pinterest, 2026 — LLMs Need Encoders for Semantic IDs Too (PrefixMem).
  • Formal, Piwowarski and Clinchant, 2021 — SPLADE — 0.322 versus 0.312 MRR@10 on MS MARCO.
  • Kasalický et al., RecSys 2025 — The Future is Sparse: Embedding Compression for Scalable Retrieval in Recommender Systems — CompresSAE; code at github.com/recombee/CompresSAE.
  • Recombee Labs — 2025 research retrospective — "dense embeddings are a convenient default, but at scale they become one of the most expensive parts".
TakeawayBefore paying for a larger model, ask whether the task has structure the model is not being given. A small model with the right encoder, or a sparse representation with the right index, often does the job for a fraction of the cost.

013Recommenders learn what matters to each user

What this slide shows

Three families of recommendation signal, with what each does, when it works and where it fails. Content-based methods match item attributes to a profile and work for new items that have text, images or metadata, but tend to repeat what the person already consumed. Collaborative filtering learns from user–item interactions, works when many users reveal shared patterns, and fails at cold start. The neural hybrid embeds history, items and context, retrieves candidates and then ranks them; it handles large catalogues that need several signals at once, at the price of serving that needs memory and scheduling under control. The closing line: recommendation is retrieval conditioned on a person.

Concepts worth knowing

  • Content-based recommendation uses what an item is: its description, category, image. It can recommend a brand-new item, but only ever things that look like what you already chose.
  • Collaborative filtering uses what people do: which items were consumed together, by whom. It discovers non-obvious connections and needs no descriptions, but a new item or a new user with no history is invisible to it (cold start).
  • A hybrid combines both; modern systems do so by embedding everything, people, items and context, into one vector space, then retrieving candidates and ranking them, the same two stages as search.
  • Context means the circumstances of the request: time, device, location, the page being viewed, the session so far. The same person wants different things on Monday morning and Saturday night.

The argument

This is the recommender counterpart of slide 10. The flow in the figure, a person's interactions into one encoder, item features into another, nearest candidates into a ranker, is the retrieve-then-rank pipeline the search slides built, with the person's history standing in for the query. The column to read is "works when": the choice between families is decided by what data the catalogue actually has, which is also the lesson of the evaluation work later in the talk, where the best encoder depended on the catalogue.

Evidence and further reading

  • Hidasi et al., 2016 — GRU4Rec, Kang and McAuley, 2018 — SASRec and Sun et al., 2019 — BERT4Rec — the sequence models that took recurrent networks and then the Transformer from language and put item identifiers in place of words.
  • Naumov et al., 2019 — DLRM — the industrial feature-cross architecture that still ranks in much of production.

TakeawayContent tells you what an item is; behaviour tells you what people do with it. A system needs both, and it should know which one it is missing for a given item or person.

014User-based collaborative filtering: borrow votes from similar users

What this slide shows

The mechanism in four steps: observe user–item interactions, compare users by the overlap of their histories, aggregate the neighbours' scores weighted by similarity, and recommend the items the target person has not seen, ranked. The prediction is a baseline for the target person plus the similarity-weighted opinions of the neighbourhood. The limit is stated on the slide: sparse histories and new users produce weak neighbourhoods.

Concepts worth knowing

  • The interaction matrix is the table with one row per user and one column per item, a mark where an interaction happened. In real systems it is more than 99.9 % empty, which is what sparsity means here.
  • User–user similarity measures how much two rows overlap; the neighbourhood is the handful of most similar users. Their behaviour is treated as a vote about what the target person might want.
  • The item-based variant flips the table and asks which items are consumed together; "people who bought this also bought" is that method, and it is what large shops actually run because item relationships change more slowly than user profiles.

The argument

One classical algorithm, taught fully, so that "collaborative filtering" is not a black box for the rest of the talk. Two properties matter later. The method needs no item text at all, which is its strength on established catalogues and its weakness at cold start. And its item-based form is the lineage that leads to the linear models on slide 24, which are in effect a learned, regularised version of this neighbourhood.

Evidence and further reading

  • Resnick et al., 1994 — GroupLens: An Open Architecture for Collaborative Filtering of Netnews — the original user-based method.
  • Linden, Smith and York, 2003 — Amazon.com Recommendations: Item-to-Item Collaborative Filtering — the item-based variant that made neighbourhoods precomputable at scale.
  • Steck, 2019 — Embarrassingly Shallow Autoencoders for Sparse Data (EASE) — the closed-form model that turns the item neighbourhood into a learned matrix.
  • Schein et al., 2002 — Methods and Metrics for Cold-Start Recommendations — the cold-start limit, measured.
TakeawayPeople who agreed with you before become voters on what you may want next. The method is simple, needs no descriptions, and goes blind exactly when there is no history to compare.

017LLM as a recommender: A language model is not a catalogue

What this slide shows

Three columns. Left, the failure: a request goes into a language model, which analyses it and generates an item that has no catalogue ID. Middle, what goes wrong in production when the model is the recommender: plausible titles with no matching ID, stale prices and availability recalled from memory, user history missed unless it is in the prompt, no collaborative signal from similar users, token-by-token candidate generation that adds latency and bypasses retrieval, fluent rationales that hide errors, and no business rules. Right, the solution: separate the jobs. The language model interprets; the recommender retrieves and ranks valid IDs from the catalogue, the person's history and collaborative signals; the language model explains what came back. The closing line: let the model choose the words, not invent the inventory.

Concepts worth knowing

  • Hallucination is a language model producing confident content that is not true. In recommendation it usually takes a specific form: an item that does not exist, is out of stock, or breaks a rule, described in perfectly plausible words.
  • A catalogue ID is the identifier your inventory system knows an item by. A model that outputs names instead of IDs has not recommended anything your system can act on.
  • Parametric memory is what the model absorbed during training; it is frozen at that time and cannot know today's prices or stock.
  • Constrained decoding forces a generating model to produce only outputs from an allowed set, for example only identifiers that exist; it turns "rarely invents items" into "cannot invent items".

The argument

Prompting the model to be careful cannot guarantee catalogue validity; the boundary has to be enforced with identifiers and validation. The architecture on the right is retrieval-augmented generation with a recommender, rather than a document index, as the source of truth. The literature agrees on the middle column's most important row: language-model recommenders without collaborative knowledge lose to plain collaborative filtering on items that have history, because a prompt cannot see what thousands of similar users did.

Evidence and further reading

  • Lewis et al., 2020 — Retrieval-Augmented Generation — generate over retrieved evidence, here over retrieved items.
  • Kim et al., KDD 2024 — A-LLMRec — language-model recommenders beat collaborative filtering on new items and are beaten by it on items with history; the paper's fix aligns the language model with a frozen collaborative model.
  • Koren, Bell and Volinsky, 2009 — Matrix Factorization Techniques — the collaborative signal a prompt cannot see.
  • Rajput et al., NeurIPS 2023 — Recommender Systems with Generative Retrieval (TIGER) — the other route to a generative recommender that cannot invent items: generate semantic IDs under a prefix tree of real items.
  • Wu et al. — RAG survey — the named failure modes of retrieval-augmented systems and faithfulness to evidence as the metric.
TakeawayGive the language model the words and the recommender the inventory. A system that lets the model name items will eventually name one you do not have.

019Unfold the vector: sparse autoencoders make a profile legible

What this slide shows

The offline part of the steering pipeline from Jaroslav Hradil's 2026 master's thesis at CTU. Train a collaborative-filtering model (ALS, ELSA, LightGCN or a two-tower model) and expose it as two functions: encode a person's interactions into a dense vector, and decode a vector into item scores. Fit a TopK sparse autoencoder to the dense user vectors: a latent two to eight times wider than the vector, a ReLU, and only the k largest activations kept, with k of 16 or 32. Learn a mapper from text vectors into that sparse latent, using item metadata or synthetic requests. At inference, map a natural-language request into a latent adjustment, add it to the person's sparse code, then decode through the autoencoder and the recommender. The reconstruction check: at 4,096 latent dimensions, increasing k moves the quality through the autoencoder path toward the recommender's own.

Concepts worth knowing

  • A dense user vector is polysemantic: one coordinate may mean "science fiction and documentary and adventure" at once, because the model packed several notions into it. A sparse autoencoder unfolds it into a much wider vector in which most coordinates mean one thing and only a few are active for any one person; those coordinates can then be addressed individually.
  • TopK keeps only the k largest activations and zeroes the rest; it is how the sparsity is enforced. A ReLU is the simplest neural non-linearity: negative values become zero.
  • ALS, ELSA, LightGCN and two-tower are four different families of collaborative model; the method is called backbone-agnostic because it wraps any of them without retraining them.
  • NDCG (normalised discounted cumulative gain) is a standard ranking metric: it rewards putting the truly relevant items near the top, with diminishing credit further down. The reconstruction check asks whether NDCG through the autoencoder stays close to the recommender's own.

The argument

Two tools from the language-model world, transplanted. Sparse autoencoders were built to read the internals of large language models by unfolding tangled directions into mostly single-meaning features; here they read the internals of a collaborative filter. Nothing in the underlying recommender is retrained, which is what makes the method work across four different models. The reconstruction check is the licence to do this at all: the sparse code loses little of the recommendation quality.

Evidence and further reading

  • Hradil, 2026 — master's thesis, CTU FIT, supervised by Rodrigo Alves and computed on Recombee infrastructure; listed at recombee.com/research.
  • Vančura, Alves, Kasalický and Kordík, RecSys 2022 — Scalable Linear Shallow Autoencoder for Collaborative Filtering (ELSA) — one of the four backbones, and the strongest on the news dataset.
  • Kasalický et al., RecSys 2025 — CompresSAE — the lab's other use of sparse autoencoders, on item vectors rather than user vectors.
TakeawayA recommender's picture of you is a tangle of numbers; a sparse autoencoder unfolds it into a few named knobs without retraining anything. That is the precondition for letting a sentence turn one of them.

020Say it: a sentence becomes a direction in that space

What this slide shows

The online part, and how the mapper is trained. For every item a language model writes five short requests that describe it without naming it, "animated buddy adventure for all ages" for Toy Story; the requests are split three, one and one per item into training, validation and test, so the test asks whether a new phrasing about a known item moves that item. A two-layer mapper learns from the text vector (Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B) to the item's sparse code, trained with InfoNCE, a retrieval objective. Online, the request is embedded, mapped to a direction δ, normalised to the person's activation mass, blended as (1−α)·user + α·δ, TopK re-applied, then decoded through the autoencoder and the recommender. Mass is normalised to avoid score-scale drift; the effect on ranking quality is measured, not assumed. No retraining anywhere.

Concepts worth knowing

  • A text embedding model turns a sentence into a vector; Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B is a small open one built for search. The steering request is, literally, a search query.
  • InfoNCE is a contrastive training objective: pull the vector of a request toward the vector of its item and push it away from the other items in the batch. It is the recipe behind dense retrieval and behind CLIP's image–text matching.
  • Synthetic requests stand in for the requests real users would type, which do not exist yet for a new mechanism. Writing them from item metadata is the same trick that HyDE uses in search, where a model writes a hypothetical document for a query.
  • α is the strength of steering, from 0 (the profile unchanged) to 1 (the profile fully replaced by the request); a product would expose it as a slider.
  • MRR here is the reciprocal rank of the requested item; a rise from 0.042 to 0.324 is, in reciprocal-rank terms, a move from about 24th place to about 3rd.

The argument

This is where the convergence thesis shows up inside the lab's own work: the recommender is steered by the machinery of semantic search, a text embedder, a contrastive objective and synthetic queries, pointed at the user vector instead of at documents. The results from the thesis: on the MIND news dataset with the ELSA backbone the requested item's mean reciprocal rank rises from 0.042 to 0.324; on MovieLens-25M with ALS it roughly doubles, 0.111 to 0.216. Two controls matter. Steering toward a random other item lowers the target's rank, so the effect is meaning rather than mixing; and without the sparse autoencoder the same mapper reaches only 0.144 instead of 0.235 on MIND. The limits belong beside the numbers: an offline study with no user study, a probe that credits only the exact item, additive requests only ("less action" is not handled yet), and a test built from paraphrases about known items.

Evidence and further reading

  • Hradil, 2026 — master's thesis, CTU FIT; Tables 5.1–5.4 and the ablations behind the numbers above; listed at recombee.com/research.
  • Reimers and Gurevych, 2019 — Sentence-BERT and Karpukhin et al., 2020 — DPR — the lineage of the text embedder and of contrastive training with in-batch negatives; Radford et al., 2021 — CLIP for the same objective across images and text.
  • Geng et al., 2022 — P5 and D'Amico et al., 2026 — GLIDE — the interface-level and generator-level alternatives the thesis is positioned against.
TakeawayA sentence can move a recommender's picture of you, using nothing but search machinery and no retraining; moderate steering enriches the profile with the topic, full steering overwrites it, and the dial in between is the product.

021What a query costs at a hundred million users

What this slide shows

The budget nobody negotiates. Kuaishou states its recommender's envelope as more than 400,000 requests per second under 500 milliseconds; an example synchronous surface has about 100 milliseconds end to end, shared by retrieval, features, ranking, constraints and the network; a language model on the request path costs thousands of tokens per call. The arithmetic, at September 2026 list prices for a hosted model: 100 million users, three sessions and five pages a day make 1.5 billion ranking calls; at 1,000 tokens a call and ten cents per million tokens, the generation step alone is $150,000 a day, before retrieval and before any cache hit. A dot product costs microseconds.

Concepts worth knowing

  • QPS (queries per second) is the load a system must carry at peak; 400,000 per second is the scale of a national short-video platform.
  • Latency budget is the time one request is allowed to take, typically stated for the slow tail (the 95th or 99th percentile), not the average; a page that is fast on average and slow for one user in twenty is a slow page.
  • Tokens are what language models are priced in, both for the text sent and the text produced; a request with a long history costs more every time the history is resent.
  • A dot product is the multiplication of two vectors into one number, the operation that scores an item against a user in a vector model; it costs a few nanoseconds per item.
  • A cache hit is a request answered from stored results instead of recomputed; the arithmetic on the slide assumes none, which is the worst case.

The argument

The cost axis in one sentence of arithmetic. Cost per request is a learning objective, not an operations detail: in Recombee's own taxonomy of recommender objectives, cost efficiency and sub-100-millisecond response sit among the goals of the learning problem. The Kuaishou quotation is chosen because it is a production team saying, in its own report, that recommenders "are often constrained to operate at a low scale and are not computation-intensive" because of exactly this envelope. Self-hosting changes the numbers; it does not change the shape.

Evidence and further reading

  • OneRec Technical Report, Kuaishou 2025 — the 400,000 QPS, under-500 ms envelope.
  • Modern Recommender Systems, Part 3: Objectives — speed and cost efficiency as objectives of the learning problem.
  • Kwon et al., 2023 — PagedAttention and Yu et al., 2022 — Orca — why the token cost is also a latency and scheduling cost.
TakeawayMultiply your users by their pages by the cost of one model call before you design the system. A vector lookup and a language-model call differ by six orders of magnitude, and the request path is where that difference is paid.

022Compute versus value

What this slide shows

Two systems that claim a scaling law for recommendation, and their bills. Meta's HSTU: 1.5 trillion parameters, a model "three orders of magnitude more computationally complex than prior state-of-the-art", and in the paper's own words a 12.4 % improvement in online A/B metrics, the metric not named; affordable because a serving algorithm, M-FALCON, amortises a 285× more complex model at 1.5–3× throughput. Kuaishou's OneRec: one end-to-end model replacing the four-stage cascade at about ten times its arithmetic, App Stay Time up 0.54 % and 1.24 % on the two apps, and operating expense at 10.6 % of the old pipeline, because utilisation of the accelerators rose to 23.7 % in training and 28.8 % in inference where the cascade had left them idle. The closing line: in Kuaishou's accounting the saving came from using the hardware, not from a smarter model, and the migration cost is not in the report.

Concepts worth knowing

  • A scaling law is the empirical finding, first in language models, that quality improves predictably as a straight line on log scales when training compute grows. The claim here is that recommendation models follow one too, once they are built like language models.
  • An A/B test shows two versions of a system to two random groups of real users and compares a business metric; it is the only measurement the whole industry accepts, and the slide's numbers are its outputs. App Stay Time is one such metric: how long people stay in the app.
  • A cascade is the multi-stage pipeline of slide 6 (retrieve, pre-rank, rank); an end-to-end model replaces the stages with one model trained for the final outcome.
  • MFU (model FLOPs utilisation) is the share of an accelerator's arithmetic capacity that useful model work actually uses; the rest is lost to data movement and waiting. OPEX is operating expense, the running cost.

The argument

Both papers say the scaling law of language models holds for recommendation; the fine print holds the systems lesson. A platform with billions of users can afford a thousandfold model complexity for twelve percent, because one percent of billions is a large number, an economic intuition rather than a result from the paper. Kuaishou's message is more useful to most organisations: fragmenting the work into stages wasted the accelerators, so a single model at ten times the arithmetic cost a tenth as much to run. Neither paper reports what rebuilding the stack cost, and nobody has.

Evidence and further reading

  • Zhai et al., ICML 2024 — Actions Speak Louder than Words: Trillion-Parameter Sequential Transducers for Generative Recommendations (HSTU) — recommendation reformulated as sequence modelling, the scaling-law figure, the 12.4 % and the M-FALCON serving algorithm.

023Four serving levers

What this slide shows

Four engineering ideas that made large recommendation models fit the budget. Cache the person's state across requests with a hierarchical key-value cache spanning GPU and host memory (MTServe: up to 3.1× faster, hit ratio above 98.5 %). Reuse the encoding across stages so retrieval and ranking share one cache (UniPinRec at Pinterest: more than 3× faster ranking passes, 11.1 % lower end-to-end latency). Deduplicate attention over the shared user sequence (PinFM: 600 % more serving throughput, 200 % more in training). Replace history self-attention with linear target-to-history cross-attention and batch by request for 10,000-event histories (Douyin: 77–84 % less bandwidth, at full traffic). The closing line: at scale a modelling win is realised only together with a serving win.

Concepts worth knowing

  • The user state is the encoding of a person's history that the model computes before scoring candidates; it is the expensive part of every request and the same across the many candidates and stages of one request, which is why it can be cached, shared and deduplicated.
  • Self-attention over a history of n events costs n² comparisons; cross-attention from a candidate to the history costs n, which is what makes 10,000-event histories affordable.
  • Batching groups requests or candidates so the accelerator works on many at once; request-level batching groups everything that belongs to one person so their history is encoded a single time.
  • Bandwidth is the rate at which data moves between memory and the accelerator; at this scale it, not arithmetic, is usually the bottleneck.

The argument

Every row is a serving idea that unlocked a modelling result; without the kernel or the cache, the model would not have shipped. The pattern for anyone building such a system: the person's history is the expensive part of every request, so cache it, share it between stages and never encode it twice. At scale the model and the serving are designed together, which is why the systems section of these papers is the one to read first.

Evidence and further reading

  • Wang et al., 2026 — MTServe: Efficient Serving for Generative Recommendation Models with Hierarchical Caches.
  • Mantha et al., 2026 — UniPinRec: Unifying Generative Retrieval and Ranking at Pinterest Scale — also the paper in which a 14.8 % offline gain became about 1 % online, and the system shipped on latency and throughput.
  • Rajesh et al., RecSys 2025 — PinFM — the deduplicated cross-attention kernel.
  • Zhao et al., WWW 2026 — Make It Long, Keep It Fast: End-to-End 10k-Sequence Modeling at Billion Scale on Douyin.

024Linear models are not dead

What this slide shows

EASE against ELSA. EASE, from Netflix in 2019: a dense item–item matrix in closed form, negative weights allowed, the strongest simple baseline in the field, and quadratic memory, ten million items squared at 32 bits is about 400 terabytes of weights, so it never starts on a large catalogue. ELSA, from Recombee Labs at RecSys 2022: the hidden layer factorised into a low-rank plus sparse part, memory linear in the catalogue, the item–item matrix never materialised, more accurate than EASE on several tasks, validated at industrial scale in ACM Transactions on Recommender Systems 2025, in production for catalogues of tens of millions of items, with a deployed successor, SANSA. The closing line: keep a strong linear baseline.

Concepts worth knowing

  • An item–item model scores an item for a person as a weighted sum over the items in their history; the weights form a matrix with one row and one column per item. It is the learned form of "people who consumed this also consumed that".
  • A closed-form solution is one computed by a formula in one step rather than by iterative training; EASE is a single matrix inversion.
  • Quadratic memory means the cost grows with the square of the catalogue: doubling the items quadruples the matrix. Low-rank means a large matrix is represented as the product of two thin ones; sparse means most entries are zero and only the rest are stored. ELSA uses both to avoid ever building the full matrix.
  • A baseline is the simple method any new model must beat under identical conditions; overfitting is a model learning the noise in its training data rather than the pattern, and linear models are hard to overfit.

The argument

The line from the field's reproducibility crisis to the lab. The methods that kept beating neural recommenders in the 2019 study were linear and neighbourhood-based, and the reason nobody deployed the best of them was a quadratic matrix. ELSA removes the matrix, scales linearly in the catalogue and is more accurate, not merely cheaper; a 2026 study of diffusion recommenders found the same pattern again, with a 25-year-old ItemKNN matching the newest models. Linear models are hard to overfit, which on sparse, high-dimensional interaction data is an advantage rather than a limitation.

Evidence and further reading

  • Steck, 2019 — Embarrassingly Shallow Autoencoders for Sparse Data — EASE.
  • Vančura, Alves, Kasalický and Kordík, RecSys 2022 — Scalable Linear Shallow Autoencoder for Collaborative Filtering — ELSA; the industrial-scale evaluation followed in ACM TORS 2025, listed at recombee.com/research.
  • Ferrari Dacrema, Cremonesi and Jannach, RecSys 2019 — Are We Really Making Much Progress? — of 18 neural methods, 7 could be reproduced and 6 of those were beaten by tuned simple baselines; extended in arXiv:1911.07698.
  • Benigni, Ferrari Dacrema and Jannach, ACM TORS 2026 — Diffusion Recommender Models and the Illusion of Progress — only 25 % of reported results reproducible; DiffRec at the level of ItemKNN.
  • Recombee — Linear Methods and Autoencoders in Recommender Systems — the SLIM → EASE → ELSA line explained.
TakeawayPut a strong, tuned linear model in every comparison. Twice in seven years the field found that most of its newest models did not beat one, and the one that could not be deployed only needed its matrix factorised.

What this slide shows

The mechanism from the lab's RecSys 2024 paper. A sentence Transformer of the Sentence-BERT family encodes each item's text; the ELSA objective reconstructs users' interaction vectors through an item–item operator built from those text vectors; and the gradient of that objective flows back into the encoder. The result is one encoder whose vectors know both what an item is and what people do with it, which gives cold-start and zero-shot recommendation and transfer across domains, train on books and recommend films. Training on several domains accumulates knowledge in one model. The closing line: ELSA stopped being only a model and became a loss function you can use to train something else entirely.

Concepts worth knowing

  • A sentence Transformer is a language model fine-tuned so that one vector per text is meaningful on its own; it is the standard encoder behind semantic search.
  • Gradient flow is how a neural network learns: the error at the output is traced back through every layer to adjust the weights. Letting the recommendation error flow into the text encoder means the encoder itself learns what people do, not only what things are called.
  • Zero-shot means working on a catalogue the model was never trained on; transfer means knowledge learned on one domain helps on another.
  • Semantic similarity is closeness of descriptions; interaction similarity is closeness of consumption. Two products can be described alike and bought by different people, or described differently and bought together.

The argument

Several industrial labs reached the same pattern with large language models: Xiaohongshu's NoteLLM lets a 7-billion-parameter model both embed and generate notes, and Recformer treats an item as a sentence of its attributes. beeFormer does it with a small sentence encoder and a linear loss, which is what makes it trainable on ordinary hardware. It also explains the observation behind slide 13: an off-the-shelf text embedding from a retrieval leaderboard knows similarity of descriptions, not of consumption, and the interaction loss is how the encoder learns the second.

Evidence and further reading

  • Vančura, Kordík and Straka, RecSys 2024 — beeFormer: Bridging the Gap Between Semantic and Interaction Similarity in Recommender Systems — the paper; code at github.com/recombee/beeformer.
  • Reimers and Gurevych, 2019 — Sentence-BERT — the encoder family beeFormer trains.
  • Vančura et al., RecSys 2022 — ELSA — the objective.
  • Zhang et al., 2024 — NoteLLM: A Retrievable Large Language Model for Note Recommendation and Li et al., 2023 — Text Is All You Need (Recformer) — the same idea at language-model scale.
  • Hou et al. — BLaIR — the benchmark that shows why a frozen language-model encoder alone misses collaborative signal.
TakeawayA text encoder can be taught what people do, not only what things are called, by letting the recommender's error flow into it; that one change is what makes new items recommendable from their description.

026Our REEF protocol, evaluation of recsys foundational models

What this slide shows

Results from Tomáš Laurin's 2026 master's thesis at CTU. REEF, the Recommender Embedding Evaluation Framework, holds the data loader, the task, the downstream ranker and the evaluator fixed so that only the encoder changes; eight encoders including a non-semantic baseline, three catalogues and five scenarios give 120 evaluation cells. The table reports Recall@10 for the multimodal BGE-M3 + SigLIP2 encoder, frozen against fine-tuned on interactions: MovieLens 20M 0.301 → 0.349 (+15.9 %), H&M Fashion 0.164 → 0.211 (+28.7 %), Amazon Reviews 2023 0.121 → 0.154 (+27.3 %), with cold-start gains of 13.7 %, 23.4 % and 39.0 %. LoRA beat full fine-tuning everywhere; on Amazon, full fine-tuning was worse than leaving the encoder frozen (0.121 → 0.115). ELSA beat BPR, InfoNCE, Jaccard and PMI as the training objective, and ELSA combined with InfoNCE collapsed (0.323 → 0.245). The honest negative: four of six cross-domain and cross-dataset transfer settings stayed at the random-ranker floor. The winning configuration in the figure: LoRA, interaction-conditioned attention fusion of text and image, and the ELSA reconstruction loss.

Concepts worth knowing

  • A foundation model is a large model pretrained on broad data and adapted to many tasks; a backbone or encoder is the pretrained part that turns text or images into vectors. BGE-M3 is a text encoder, SigLIP2 an image–text encoder; multimodal means both are used together.
  • Recall@10 is the share of the items a person actually went on to interact with that appear in the top ten recommended; here it is computed against 999 randomly sampled other items, a common shortcut that makes the numbers comparable within the study rather than across the literature.
  • Fine-tuning adapts a pretrained model to new data by continuing training; LoRA (low-rank adaptation) does so by training a small add-on while the original weights stay frozen, which is cheaper and, as this study shows, safer.
  • BPR, InfoNCE, Jaccard, PMI are alternative training objectives, ways of telling the encoder what counts as a good item vector; ELSA won because it aligns the vector geometry with collaborative behaviour while keeping the pretrained semantics.
  • Fusion is how the text and image vectors of one item are combined; interaction-conditioned attention lets the model weigh them per item, guided by which items were consumed together.

The argument

The thesis names a problem the field had not isolated, the backbone isolation problem: when a pretrained encoder is judged inside a whole pipeline, its merit is confounded with the ranker, the training budget and the data split, so two teams comparing encoders disagree and neither is wrong. The answer is a protocol, not a model; Snap's GRID handbook reached the same diagnosis for generative recommenders in the same year and also answered with a framework. The fine-tuning result generalises beeFormer to text plus image, and finds that the 2022 linear loss aligns the vector space with collaborative behaviour while keeping enough semantic structure for cold start. The negative result is part of the finding: transfer to a disjoint catalogue did not work, and a method that says so is more useful than one that does not.

Evidence and further reading

  • Laurin, 2026 — Training and Evaluation of Foundation Models for Recommender Systems, master's thesis, CTU FIT, supervised by Pavel Kordík; listed at recombee.com/research.
  • Ju et al., CIKM 2025 — Generative Recommendation with Semantic IDs: A Practitioner's Handbook (GRID, Snap) — "varied modeling techniques, hyper-parameters and experimental setups make direct comparisons challenging"; the same disease one floor up.
  • Chen et al., 2024 — BGE M3-Embedding and Radford et al., 2021 — CLIP — the text and image–text encoder families in the study.
  • Vančura et al., RecSys 2022 — ELSA and Vančura, Kordík and Straka, RecSys 2024 — beeFormer — the objective and its first use inside an encoder.
  • Kim et al., KDD 2024 — A-LLMRec and Rajput et al., NeurIPS 2023 — TIGER — the cold/warm pattern that REEF's cold-start scenario confirms: without content the baseline falls to the random floor, every pretrained encoder stays above it.
TakeawayYou cannot say which encoder is better until everything else is held still. Held still, a frozen encoder with a small adapter and the right objective gained 16–29 % — and the objective that won was a linear loss from 2022.

028Convergence merges vulnerabilities: SHIELD

What this slide shows

The failure and the fix. Semantic retrieval understands meaning, so a query such as "glass tubing" passes a keyword blocklist and returns laboratory equipment with an obvious illicit use; a harmful request phrased without any blocked word is understood and answered. SHIELD, from Recombee Labs at UMAP 2025, is a three-stage pipeline: harmful and sensitive queries generated by a language model category by category, filtered by a reward model for realism, and used to train a classifier. On a marketplace test set of 17,170 harmful and 8,871 sensitive queries, BM25 keyword similarity reaches 93.2 % accuracy, semantic embeddings with a vector index 96.5 %, and the fine-tuned MoralBERT 98.4 % (F1 98.6 %). The closing line: merge the stacks and you merge the attack surface; the fix has to understand meaning as well as the search does.

Concepts worth knowing

  • A keyword blocklist refuses queries containing listed words; it protected marketplaces for a decade because keyword search could only find what the words said.
  • A classifier is a model that assigns a label, here harmful, sensitive or neither, to a query; accuracy is the share of labels it gets right, F1 balances how many harmful queries it catches against how many innocent ones it wrongly blocks.
  • A reward model is a model trained to score how good or realistic a text is; here it filters the generated training queries so that only convincing ones are kept.

The argument

The lab's own evidence that convergence is not free. The moment search became semantic, the keyword blocklists stopped working, because the retriever matches intent rather than strings. The remedy has to live at the same level, a classifier trained on semantically generated data, sitting in front of retrieval. The dataset itself is the reusable part; the classifier is one instance of what it makes possible.

Evidence and further reading

  • SHIELD: the universal framework making AI search safer for everyone — the pipeline and the evaluation; presented at UMAP 2025 and listed with Mitigating Risks in Online Semantic Search at recombee.com/research.
  • Karpukhin et al., 2020 — DPR and Khattab and Zaharia, 2020 — ColBERT — the semantic retrievers whose strength is the problem here.
TakeawayWhen you upgrade search to understand meaning, upgrade the safety layer to understand it too; a blocklist written for keywords is a door left open.

029Exposure and the feedback loop

What this slide shows

The model learns only from what it showed. A recommend → click → retrain loop amplifies popularity bias, shrinks catalogue coverage and homogenises profiles, and the minority user group loses the most precision. Seven named biases enter at three points, data, model and results, and the loop feeds them back. Diversification from web search, xQuAD, and fair top-k ranking are reused as recommender re-rankers, which shows the post-processing slot is shared across interfaces. And the lab's minimum item exposure guarantees give eligible items an exposure floor with a measured relevance trade-off.

Concepts worth knowing

  • The feedback loop: a recommender shows items, people click among what was shown, the clicks become training data, the retrained model shows more of the same. Nothing outside the shown set can ever be learned about.
  • Popularity bias is the resulting drift toward already-popular items; coverage is the share of the catalogue that gets recommended at all, and it shrinks under the loop.
  • Re-ranking is a final step that reorders a ranked list to satisfy a constraint, diversity, fairness or a minimum exposure, at a small cost in raw relevance. xQuAD is a re-ranker originally built to diversify web search results.
  • A relevance trade-off is the measured price of a constraint: how much predicted engagement is given up to guarantee that every eligible item is shown at least sometimes.

The argument

An alignment topic anyone running a catalogue can act on: exposure is a resource the system allocates, and a floor on it is a constraint that can be enforced at re-ranking time. Two things stand out. The minority-user result turns "bias" from an abstraction into a measured loss for identifiable people; and the shared post-processing slot is convergence again, a web-search diversification algorithm running unchanged inside a recommender.

Evidence and further reading

  • Mansoury, 2021 — Understanding and Mitigating Multi-Sided Exposure Bias in Recommender Systems — the feedback-loop simulation and the minority-user finding.
  • Chen et al. — Bias and Debias in Recommender System: A Survey and Future Directions — the taxonomy.
  • Making recommendations fairer: a new way to guarantee exposure for all — the lab's minimum-exposure method and its trade-off curve.
  • Zhu et al., SIGIR 2023 — Popularity Debiasing from Exposure to Interaction in Collaborative Filtering — the same loop treated at the interaction level.
TakeawayBias is not a property of the data you collected; it is a property of the loop your system closes on itself. Exposure is the resource that loop allocates, and you can put a floor under it.

030Evaluation is alignment: judges need behavioural alignment too

What this slide shows

Bidirectional rationalisation, a named failure mode: used zero-shot, a language model "convincingly argues for both positive and negative user engagement outcomes on the exact same item with identical evidence". Fine-tuning with preference optimisation over paired correct and counterfactual rationales lifts MacroF1 on real homepage interaction logs by 32.19 % over the zero-shot judge and matches the production feature-engineered model. A simulator earns trust the same way: ContextSim is validated against 55 real A/B experiments, not against its own plausibility. The closing line: a judge that can argue either way is not a judge; align it to behaviour, then measure the correlation. The figure shows why both evaluation bridges, synthetic users and model judges, are circular unless anchored outside the model.

Concepts worth knowing

  • LLM-as-a-judge means asking a language model to predict or rate what a person would do, instead of running an experiment with people; it is cheap and, unaligned, unreliable.
  • Zero-shot use means asking the model without any training on the task. Preference optimisation trains it on pairs of a correct and an incorrect rationale so that it learns which side the evidence actually supports.
  • MacroF1 averages the F1 score over classes so that rare outcomes count as much as common ones.
  • Offline evaluation uses logged data and metrics such as NDCG; online evaluation is an A/B test with real users; the gap between the two is the reason judges and simulators exist, and a Spearman correlation with real A/B outcomes is how a simulator proves it can stand in for them.

The argument

The bridge between the accuracy and the alignment axes. The tools that promise to replace offline metrics, language-model judges and agent simulators, have to be aligned to observed behaviour and checked against A/B outcomes before they are allowed to rank models, because they inherit the assumptions of the model that powers them. The failure mode now has a name, which makes it something to ask about. Behind this sits an older finding: offline metrics computed on sampled data can mis-rank methods and, as the judges paper puts it, frequently fail to correlate strongly with online results.

Evidence and further reading

  • Ziabari et al., 2026 — From Prompting to Behavioral Alignment: Personalized LLM Judges for Recommendation Evaluation — bidirectional rationalisation and the +32.19 % MacroF1.
  • Bougie et al., 2026 — Beyond Offline A/B Testing: Context-Aware Agent Simulation for Recommender System Evaluation (ContextSim) — a simulator scored by its correlation with 55 historical A/B tests.
  • Ferrari Dacrema, Cremonesi and Jannach, RecSys 2019 and Benigni, Ferrari Dacrema and Jannach, ACM TORS 2026 — why the offline number is a hypothesis: weak baselines and irreproducible results, twice, seven years apart.
  • Mantha et al., 2026 — UniPinRec — the production example of the gap: a 14.8 % offline gain became about 1 % online, and the system shipped on latency and throughput.
TakeawayAn offline number is a hypothesis, an A/B test is the measurement, and anything you put in between, a judge or a simulator, has to be calibrated against real outcomes before it is allowed to decide.
