003How information retrieval evolved over time?

What the slide says

A timeline in four eras: retrieving records (Boolean retrieval), ranking documents (BM25, PageRank), learning representations (word2vec, BERT) and generating responses (retrieval-augmented generation, generative search). Two questions close it: is generative AI part of information retrieval, and what exactly is IR in 2026?

The argument

The eras are defined by what the system could score, not by technology fashion. Boolean systems matched; probabilistic ranking ordered; learned representations made similarity a geometric relation between vectors; the generative era retrieves evidence and composes an answer over it. The important observation for the rest of the talk is that the last step did not replace retrieval, it wrapped it: a generated answer still stands on a ranked list of evidence. The two closing questions are answered by the talk itself, and the answer is that IR in 2026 is the selection and ordering of items, whatever the interface then does with them.

Evidence and further reading

  • Mikolov et al., 2013 — Efficient Estimation of Word Representations in Vector Space — word2vec, the moment "similar" became "nearby in a vector space".
  • Devlin et al., 2018 — BERT — contextual representations from one pretrained model, reused across tasks.
  • Lewis et al., 2020 — Retrieval-Augmented Generation — the paper that coupled a generator to a retriever, which is where the fourth era starts.
  • Robertson et al., 1994 — Okapi at TREC-3 — BM25, still the strongest lexical baseline three decades later.

005History of Search, Recommender systems and Conversational AI

What the slide says

Four lineages in a table: classical information retrieval (Luhn, Salton, BM25), web search (PageRank, HITS), recommender systems (GroupLens, Amazon's item-to-item filtering, matrix factorization) and conversational interfaces (ELIZA, seq2seq, the Transformer, RAG). For each, the signal it added and the scope of the cited work. The key line orders the signals in time: term statistics, links, user–item behaviour, learned sequence context, retrieval-grounded generation.

The argument

The point of the citation table is not erudition. It shows that four communities added four different signals to the same operation, and that each new signal came with a new candidate set: documents, then pages, then items for a person, then evidence for an answer. Salton's vector space model is the hinge: it established that documents and queries are vectors, but the similarity stayed lexical. Learned embeddings, four decades later, gave all four lineages the same representational machinery, which is why they could converge at all.

006Learning to rank added a learned ordering layer

What the slide says

Two columns. Left, classical retrieval: BM25 or TF-IDF sparse vectors, cosine similarity, and a hand-set weight α that mixes the two scores. Right, pointwise learning to rank as linear regression: the same signals become features x(q,d), a weight vector w is fitted from query–document relevance labels by squared error, and the learned model reranks the BM25 top-k candidates. The comparison table makes the shift explicit: hand-set α becomes learned coefficients; "retrieve and sort" becomes "rerank the candidates the first stage found".

The argument

This is the first mechanism slide, and it introduces the pattern the whole stack still uses: a cheap first stage that preserves recall, and a learned second stage that improves ordering by combining heterogeneous signals. The key line names the limitation that comes with it: a reranker cannot recover a document the first stage omitted. That limitation is why candidate generation is a topic of its own later, and why "retrieval quality" and "ranking quality" are measured separately in production.

Evidence and further reading

  • Robertson et al., 1994 — Okapi at TREC-3 — the lexical first stage the learned layer sits on.
  • Learning to rank as a discipline goes back to RankNet (Burges et al., 2005) and LambdaMART; the slide keeps to the pointwise linear case because it is the one an information-systems audience can rebuild from a regression they already know.
  • Karpukhin et al., 2020 — Dense Passage Retrieval — the later step where the first stage itself becomes learned; the retrieve-then-rerank shape survives it.

007The model is only half of learning to rank

What the slide says

The scoring function can be a linear regression, gradient-boosted trees (LambdaMART) or a neural ranker; that is one axis. The other axis is what the training data says: an itemwise cross-entropy loss learns one relevance probability from (query, document, relevant?) triples; a pairwise loss such as RankNet learns one score difference from (query, preferred document, other document); a listwise loss such as ListNet learns a distribution over a whole candidate list from graded labels. A worked pairwise example on the right computes the margin between a relevant and an irrelevant document and the loss it produces.

The argument

Practitioners tend to argue about model families and forget that the labels decide what the model can learn. A pairwise example supervises exactly one score difference; it says nothing about absolute relevance, and it needs relative preferences rather than yes/no judgements. Listwise training is closest to the ranking metrics people actually report. The slide's job is to make the audience read the training-data column before the model column, because that is where production ranking projects most often go wrong: click-derived labels carry position and exposure bias, whichever model consumes them.

Evidence and further reading

  • RankNet and LambdaMART are the canonical references for pairwise and listwise gradient-boosted ranking (Burges and colleagues, Microsoft Research); ListNet is Cao et al., 2007. They are named on the slide rather than linked because they are not in the verified library behind this deck.
  • Mansoury, 2021 — Understanding and Mitigating Multi-Sided Exposure Bias in Recommender Systems — why labels derived from what was shown are biased by the showing.
  • Chen et al. — Bias and Debias in Recommender System: A Survey — the taxonomy of where those biases enter.

008Transformers in search

What the slide says

Two ways to use a Transformer for ranking. A ranking Transformer, or cross-encoder, reads the query and the document together and emits one relevance score, typically from the [CLS] position; full attention between the two happens inside the encoder. ColBERT encodes query and document separately into one contextual vector per token and scores by late interaction: for each query token, the maximum similarity over document tokens, summed. The figure contrasts the two pipelines.

The argument

The cross-encoder is the most accurate thing you can do with a Transformer and the least deployable: every query–document pair costs a forward pass, so it can only rerank a short list. ColBERT's move is to keep token-level matching while pushing the document side out of the query path: documents are encoded once, offline, and the per-query work is a query encoding plus cheap MaxSim over a token index. This is the search-side instance of a pattern the recommender side had already used for years, precompute the item side, pay per request only for the cheap interaction, and it is the mechanical reason the two stacks could share machinery.

Evidence and further reading

  • Vaswani et al., 2017 — Attention Is All You Need — the encoder both variants build on; its own definition of attention is a query scored against keys, which is a ranking primitive.
  • Khattab and Zaharia, 2020 — ColBERT — late interaction over per-token embeddings; documents encoded offline, MaxSim online.
  • Reimers and Gurevych, 2019 — Sentence-BERT — the arithmetic of why cross-encoders cannot be searched: the most similar pair among 10,000 sentences costs about 50 million BERT passes, roughly 65 hours on a V100, against seconds for a siamese encoder plus vector search.
  • Formal, Piwowarski and Clinchant, 2021 — SPLADE and Chen et al., 2024 — BGE M3-Embedding — the same backbone can also feed a sparse inverted index or emit dense, sparse and multi-vector representations at once; the index becomes a deployment choice.

Explain only the diagram and the MaxSim equation.

The speaker's own noteA cross-encoder ranking Transformer jointly processes every query–document pair and emits one score. ColBERT instead stores contextual document-token vectors, encodes the query independently, and uses MaxSim followed by a sum to produce the ranking score. This reduces online computation at the cost of a larger token-level index; when short, explain only the diagram and equation.

009Search became a personalized retrieval problem

What the slide says

Two neural signals combine: query relevance from ColBERT-style token matching, and user preference from item and user embeddings learned from interaction histories. The figure shows the shared space: a user's history produces a user embedding; recommendation retrieves nearby items directly, while search shifts the same embedding with a query before retrieving. The combined score is ColBERT(q,d) plus λ times the cosine between the user and the document, with λ chosen on a held-out set per task.

The argument

This is the convergence claim made concrete. Search and recommendation differ in the request, an explicit query versus a profile, not in the representation. Two production and benchmark sources back it: Alipay trained one search-and-recommendation foundation model across seven domains, and BLaIR feeds one frozen item-embedding table into sequential recommendation, collaborative filtering and product search. The honest qualification, raised by reviewers, belongs in the spoken notes: this describes logged-in product and site search in commerce, news and super-apps; public web search remains far more constrained by privacy and by the absence of a profile.

010A query is now a hypothesis about intent

What the slide says

Semantic search as a four-step pipeline: complete the typed prefix into candidate queries (next-token prediction), understand the intent with a contextual encoder, retrieve lexical and semantic candidates (BM25 plus ColBERT), and answer with a response conditioned on the retrieved evidence (RAG). The key line: search no longer matches a string; it predicts intent, retrieves evidence and composes an answer or a ranked list.

The argument

Each step reuses a mechanism from the previous slides, which is the point: the "semantic" in semantic search is not one model but a pipeline of the same components the recommender uses. Hybrid retrieval stays in the picture on purpose, because BM25 preserves exact terms that dense encoders blur, and reviewers were right to insist on it: BM25 remains a hard zero-shot baseline in retrieval benchmarks. The last step is the door to the conversational section: an answer composed over retrieved evidence is grounded, but grounding is not correctness, which slide 17 takes up.

Evidence and further reading

  • Sutskever et al., 2014 — seq2seq and Vaswani et al., 2017 — the Transformer — the completion and understanding steps.
  • Mikolov et al., 2013 — word2vec — the static-vector baseline that contextual encoders replaced.

  • Lewis et al., 2020 — RAG — the answer step; Wu et al. — Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Natural Language Processing: A Survey — the taxonomy of how retrieved evidence is fused into the model (query-based, logits-based, latent, parametric) and the failure modes that follow.

011LLM inference is constrained by memory and scheduling

What the slide says

Transformer inference has two phases: prefill processes the prompt in parallel, decode produces one token per sequential step. The table turns that into capacity arithmetic for Llama 2 70B in BF16: about 140 GB of weights (70 billion parameters times two bytes), about 320 KiB of KV cache per token, so about 1.25 GiB per 4,096-token request; quadratic prefill attention that FlashAttention makes memory-efficient without removing the FLOPs; decode throughput that improves with batching at the cost of tail latency, which Orca addresses by scheduling at iteration granularity; and fragmented cache allocation that PagedAttention fixes, with a reported 2–4× throughput gain over FasterTransformer and Orca at comparable latency. The right column lists the limits of the usual mitigations: 4-bit weights cut the footprint to about 35 GB but GPTQ adds quantisation error; speculative decoding saves decode steps only when the draft model's guesses are accepted; continuous batching raises utilisation but latency still depends on arrival rate and output length.

The argument

The slide is the affordability axis seen from the language-model side, and it deliberately works in arithmetic rather than benchmarks. The KV-cache figure is the one to remember: 80 layers, keys and values, 8 key-value heads of dimension 128, two bytes each, is 327,680 bytes per token, so a single long conversation holds more than a gigabyte of state per request. That state is what the serving tricks on slide 23 cache, reuse and deduplicate. The speaker's note is explicit that this is capacity planning, not a measured result, and that any real number depends on the index, the hardware and the recall target.

Evidence and further reading

  • Touvron et al., 2023 — Llama 2 — the model whose sizes the arithmetic uses.
  • Dao et al., 2022 — FlashAttention — IO-aware exact attention: less memory traffic, the same FLOPs.
  • Yu et al., 2022 — Orca — iteration-level scheduling for generative serving.
  • Kwon et al., 2023 — Efficient Memory Management for LLM Serving with PagedAttention — paged KV cache; the 2–4× throughput figure.
  • Frantar et al., 2022 — GPTQ — post-training 4-bit quantisation and its error.
  • Leviathan, Kalman and Matias, 2022 — Fast Inference from Transformers via Speculative Decoding — draft-and-verify decoding.

012Structure can beat parameter count on the right task

What the slide says

Three results where structure buys more than parameters. PrefixMem at Pinterest: a 0.6-billion-parameter model with a dedicated semantic-ID encoder beats a 4-billion one without, with deepest-level semantic-ID accuracy up 46 % relative. SPLADE: a sparse inverted-index retriever trained from the same BERT slightly beats its dense sibling, 0.322 against 0.312 MRR@10. CompresSAE, from Recombee Labs: dense embeddings projected into a high-dimensional, sparsely activated space give 12× fewer parameters at near-uncompressed quality, with two inference modes, a fast one in the sparse space and an exact one in the reconstructed dense space. The right column illustrates the representation with one schematic item: eight dense values become three active coordinates out of sixteen, and back.

The argument

An earlier draft titled this "small beats big", and reviewers rightly objected that sometimes big wins. The defensible claim is the one on the slide now: on a constrained task, the right structure can dominate parameter count. Pinterest's result is the sharpest because it reuses the multimodal-LLM lesson, that a non-language modality wants its own encoder, for semantic IDs. CompresSAE is the lab's version of the same idea for retrieval: sparsity as a design decision rather than an afterthought, and a latency-versus-quality dial that can be turned at run time.

Evidence and further reading

  • Pinterest, 2026 — LLMs Need Encoders for Semantic IDs Too (PrefixMem) — the 0.6B-versus-4B result; the gain concentrates on examples where greedy decoding fails.
  • Formal, Piwowarski and Clinchant, 2021 — SPLADE — sparse lexical expansion from a BERT backbone; 0.322 versus 0.312 MRR@10 on MS MARCO.
  • Kasalický et al., RecSys 2025 — The Future is Sparse: Embedding Compression for Scalable Retrieval in Recommender Systems — CompresSAE; code at github.com/recombee/CompresSAE.

013Recommenders learn what matters to each user

What the slide says

Three families of signal, in a table that names what each does, when it works and where it fails. Content-based methods match item attributes to a profile and work for new items that have text, images or metadata, but tend to repeat what the user already consumed. Collaborative filtering learns from user–item interactions, works when many users reveal shared patterns, and fails at cold start. The neural hybrid embeds history, items and context, retrieves candidates and then ranks them; it handles large catalogues that need several signals at once, at the price of serving that needs memory and scheduling under control. The key line: recommendation is retrieval conditioned on a person.

The argument

This is the recommender counterpart of slide 10. The neural recommender flow in the figure, a user's interactions into one encoder, item features into another, nearest candidates into a ranker, is the same retrieve-then-rank pipeline the search slides built, with the person's history standing in for the query. The table's third column, "works when", is the one to read to a systems audience: the choice between families is decided by what data the catalogue actually has, which is also the lesson of the REEF thesis later in the deck, where the winning encoder depended on the catalogue.

Evidence and further reading

014User-based collaborative filtering: borrow votes from similar users

What the slide says

The mechanism in four steps: observe user–item interactions, compare users by the overlap of their histories, aggregate the neighbours' scores weighted by similarity, and recommend the items the target user has not seen, ranked. The prediction is a target-user baseline plus the similarity-weighted opinions of the neighbourhood. The limit is stated on the slide: sparse histories and new users produce weak neighbourhoods.

The argument

One classical algorithm, taught fully, so that "collaborative filtering" is not a black box for the rest of the talk. The figure is meant to be animated in four clicks, as the speaker's note says: reveal the matrix, highlight the nearest neighbours, move their weighted votes onto one unseen item, reveal the ranking. Two properties matter later. The method needs no item text at all, which is its strength on warm catalogues and its weakness at cold start; and its item-based variant, "people who bought this also bought", is the lineage that leads to the linear autoencoders (EASE, ELSA) on slide 24, which are in effect a learned, regularised version of this neighbourhood.

Evidence and further reading

  • Resnick et al., 1994 — GroupLens: An Open Architecture for Collaborative Filtering of Netnews — the original user-based method.
  • Linden, Smith and York, 2003 — Amazon.com Recommendations: Item-to-Item Collaborative Filtering — the item-based variant that made neighbourhoods precomputable at scale.
  • Steck, 2019 — Embarrassingly Shallow Autoencoders for Sparse Data (EASE) — the closed-form item–item model that turns the neighbourhood into a learned matrix.
  • Schein et al., 2002 — Methods and Metrics for Cold-Start Recommendations — the cold-start limit, measured.

019Unfold the vector: sparse autoencoders make a profile legible

What the slide says

The offline part of the steering pipeline from Jaroslav Hradil's 2026 master's thesis. Train a collaborative-filtering backbone (ALS, ELSA, LightGCN or a two-tower model) and expose it as encode(interactions) → e and decode(e) → scores. Learn a mapper from text embeddings into that sparse latent, using item metadata or synthetic requests. At inference, map a natural-language request into a latent adjustment, add it to the user's sparse code, then decode through the autoencoder and the backbone. The reconstruction check: at 4,096 latent dimensions, increasing k moves the NDCG through the autoencoder path toward the backbone's own.

The argument

Two tools from the language-model world, transplanted. Sparse autoencoders were built to read the internals of LLMs by unfolding polysemantic directions into mostly single-meaning features; here they read the internals of a collaborative filter, so that a dense coordinate meaning "science fiction and documentary and adventure at once" becomes three separate neurons a request can address. Nothing in the backbone is retrained, which is what makes the method backbone-agnostic: the thesis evaluates it on four different models. The reconstruction check is the licence to do this at all: the sparse code loses little of the downstream quality.

020Say it: a sentence becomes a direction in that space

What the slide says

The online part, and how the mapper is trained. The failure and the fix. SHIELD, from Recombee Labs at UMAP 2025, is a three-stage pipeline: hierarchical LLM-generated queries by category, reward-model filtering for relevance, and a fine-tuned classifier. On a marketplace test set of 17,170 harmful and 8,871 sensitive queries, BM25 keyword similarity reaches 93.2 % accuracy, semantic embeddings with FAISS 96.5 %, and the fine-tuned MoralBERT 98.4 % (F1 98.6 %). The key line: merge the stacks and you merge the attack surface; the fix has to understand meaning as well as the search does.

The argument

The lab's own evidence that convergence is not free. The moment search became semantic, the keyword blocklists that had protected marketplaces for a decade stopped working, because the retriever matches intent rather than strings. The remedy has to live at the same level: a classifier trained on semantically generated data, sitting in the moderation layer in front of retrieval. The SHIELD dataset itself, harmful and sensitive query alignment, is the reusable contribution; the classifier is the instance.

030Evaluation is alignment: judges need behavioural alignment too

What the slide says

Bidirectional rationalisation, a named failure mode: in zero-shot use, a language model "convincingly argues for both positive and negative user engagement outcomes on the exact same item with identical evidence". A simulator earns trust the same way: ContextSim is validated against 55 real A/B experiments, not against its own plausibility. The key line: a judge that can argue either way is not a judge; align it to behaviour, then measure the correlation. The figure shows why both evaluation bridges, synthetic users and model judges, are circular unless anchored outside the model.

The argument

The bridge between the accuracy and the alignment axes. The tools that promise to replace offline metrics, language-model judges and agent simulators, have to be aligned to observed behaviour and checked against A/B outcomes before they are allowed to rank models, because they inherit the priors of the model that powers them. The failure mode now has a name, which makes it something to ask a vendor about. Behind this sits the older finding that offline metrics on sampled negatives can mis-rank methods and, as the judges paper puts it, frequently fail to correlate strongly with online results.
